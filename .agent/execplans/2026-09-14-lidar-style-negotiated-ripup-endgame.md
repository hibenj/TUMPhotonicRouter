## Purpose / Big Picture

Our detailed router (`lidar-pure` baseline and `lidar-guided`, contribution 1) is faster than the reference router LiDAR on every benchmark up to 32 inputs, but at 64 inputs it stops finishing: on `multiportmmi_64x64` the baseline gave up after 59 minutes, on `benes_64x64` contribution 1 gave up after 64 minutes, while LiDAR routes the 64x64 mesh clean in 89 minutes. The difference is not the search. Both of our runs dispatched every net with zero search failures; they died in the repair phase, the code that decides what to do when a net's own search finds no legal route because already-routed nets are in the way. Our repair is a chain of local strategies that tries to preserve every committed route and enumerates victim sets and orderings per failure; it was built on a handful of 16x16 cases and, as the ladder numbers show (0 to 5 repairs per benchmark up to 32x32), it was never under real load before this week. LiDAR's repair is a global rip-up-and-reroute loop with history cost, the standard negotiated-congestion algorithm, and it is proven at this size.

After this plan, the router has that loop as its repair engine for the lidar modes: when a net cannot be routed legally, the nets it would cross illegally are ripped up at once and re-queued behind it; whatever still fails at the end of a pass is ripped up together with its blockers and rerouted in the next pass under a history cost; passes repeat until every net is routed or a round limit is reached. Every commit stays fully legal (no design-rule violations, ever, at any point in the output), which is the one place where this plan deliberately departs from LiDAR. The observable outcome: `python routing_flow.py multiportmmi_64x64 --crossing-mode lidar-pure` and `python routing_flow.py benes_64x64 --crossing-mode lidar-guided` finish clean within the 4 h limit with both verification reports at 0 errors, and every smaller benchmark keeps its verdicts.

## Context and Orientation

Terms used below, in plain words. A *net* is one waveguide connection between two ports; the router routes nets one after another on a 2 um cell grid and *commits* each finished route into the shared obstacle map so later nets avoid it. A *crossing* is the one legal way two waveguides intersect: a crossing cell, which the design rules only allow when the two paths meet perpendicularly and each has a straight run before and after the intersection; the checker reports the reasons `not_perpendicular` and `insufficient_straight_margin` when that is violated. *Rip-up* means deleting a committed route from the map so its net has to be routed again. A *probe* (`probe_net_for_repair` in `src/py_router.rs`) is a diagnostic search that tells, for a net that could not be routed legally, which committed nets block it (`candidate_blockers`) and which crossings would be illegal and why (`realized_reasons`). *History cost* is a per-cell price that grows every time a route through that cell had to be ripped up, so later searches drift to other corridors. A *band* is the space between two component columns of a benchmark; nets that start at the same x coordinate form a *source column*. The *lidar modes* are `--crossing-mode lidar-pure` (the baseline) and `--crossing-mode lidar-guided` (contribution 1); in both the search inserts crossings on its own where the cost function decides so.

Where things are. The kernel is Rust: `src/py_router.rs` holds the batch routers (`route_many_with_repair_and_commit`, the default 17-strategy chain, ~line 13045; `route_many_with_negotiated_repair_and_commit`, an opt-in negotiated loop from August, directly below it), the probe, `ripup_route`, `rollback_committed_route`, `commit_native_route_with_clearance`, `add_repair_history_for_route`; `src/astar.rs` is the search. Python drives it from `translation/route_rust.py`: the batch call site is `_dispatch_native_routing` (~line 4400), which picks the negotiated engine when the environment variable `PHOTONIC_ROUTER_NEGOTIATED_REPAIR=1` is set and the chain otherwise; the per-net progress and repair traces are enabled by `PHOTONIC_ROUTER_NATIVE_PROGRESS=1` and `PHOTONIC_ROUTER_NATIVE_REPAIR_DIAG=1`; `PHOTONIC_ROUTER_DEBUG_ROUTE_FIRST_NETS=<ids>` with `--debug-stop-after-route N` routes a chosen subset first and stops. Runs: `.venv/bin/python routing_flow.py <benchmark> --crossing-mode lidar-pure|lidar-guided` from the repository root; reports in `build/verification/<benchmark>_photonic_verification.json` and `_crossing_verification.json` (`error_count` must be 0, `metrics.crossing_count` is the realized crossing count). Build after Rust changes with the maturin command in `AGENTS.md`; never rebuild while a flow process runs.

What LiDAR does (from its `drgridroute.py`, so the reader need not open it). `runNRR` runs up to ten iterations. Each iteration pops nets from a priority queue; a net gets a strict-DRC search, then (not adopted here) a relaxed one. Inside `routeSingleNet`, when the found path would cross committed nets illegally and the current net has `failed_count == 0`, `ripuplocalnets` deletes those partner nets and the current net is committed; the deleted nets go back to the queue and route after it. At the end of an iteration `ripupfailedNets` deletes every net that still failed (and, via the recorded crossing partners, its blockers), raises their `failed_count`, and the next iteration reroutes them; the history map is updated on every registered route and cleared after the second global rip-up so the cost landscape does not calcify. Success is an empty queue; failure is a non-empty one after the last iteration. On the 64x64 mesh this took four iterations, 89 minutes, DRV 0 on all 895 nets, 224 crossings.

What we already have. The August engine (`route_many_with_negotiated_repair_and_commit`) implements the outer shape: a `queue` of net ids, `failed_counts`, rounds up to `max_rounds`, `commit_history_weight` set to `history_weight` only for nets with `failed_count > 0` (important: any nonzero history weight disables the JPS4 fast path of the search, so it must stay off for first attempts -- an August finding), a history reset after two rounds without progress, `try_plain_normal_route` and `probe_net_for_repair` per net, and a pairwise displacement (`try_negotiated_displacement`) gated by a slack comparison. It did not converge on `benes_16x16` because it rips up and plainly reroutes blockers without the priority rule LiDAR uses for illegal crossings, and it never re-implemented the chain's crossing-specific strategies. This plan replaces its displacement step with LiDAR's two rip-up rules and keeps everything else of it.

## Milestone 2: local rip-up of illegal crossing partners, with re-queue

Goal: LiDAR's `ripuplocalnets` rule inside the negotiated engine. When a net's plain search fails and the probe reports illegal crossings, the partner nets of those illegal crossings are ripped up immediately (each net at most once per history epoch, tracked in a `ripped_once: FxHashSet<u64>`), the current net is searched again on the freed map and committed only if that search finds a fully legal route, and the ripped partners are pushed to the front of the remaining queue with `failed_count + 1` so they route right after it. If the second search also fails, nothing is committed, the ripped partners are still re-queued, and the current net goes to the round's failed set. This replaces `try_negotiated_displacement` and its slack gate in `route_many_with_negotiated_repair_and_commit`; the old function stays in the file until Milestone 5 decides its fate.

Concretely, in `src/py_router.rs`: a method `fn ripup_illegal_crossing_partners(&mut self, batch: &mut RepairBatchState, probe: &RepairProbe, ripped_once: &mut FxHashSet<u64>) -> Vec<u64>` that collects the distinct partner ids from `probe.realized_reasons` (the tuples `(partner_id, reason)`), skips ids already in `ripped_once`, calls `self.ripup_route(id)` and removes them from `batch.final_routes`, records a trace event `"local_ripup"` with the ids via `push_native_repair_trace`, and returns the ripped ids. If the list is empty (no illegal crossing, only plain blocking), fall back to ripping the first `candidate_blockers` entry under the same once-per-epoch rule; this is the LiDAR behaviour for non-crossing conflicts and keeps the loop total. The queue becomes a `VecDeque<u64>`; ripped partners are `push_front`-ed in reverse so their relative order is preserved; the `failed_counts` bump on them is what turns history cost on for their reroute.

Tests, written first, against the Milestone 1 fixture: (1) the vertical job routes after the diagonal partner is ripped, the partner is re-queued in front, and the partner then routes again with a legal crossing; (2) a partner is ripped at most once per epoch -- a second illegal conflict with the same partner sends the current net to the failed set instead; (3) the trace contains one `local_ripup` event with the partner id. ## Milestone 3: the global round

Goal: LiDAR's `ripupfailedNets` and iteration structure. At the end of a round, every net in the failed set is ripped up together with the blockers its last probe reported; history is bumped on all their cells (`add_repair_history_for_route` already does this at every commit chokepoint; the rip-up path must call the same increment for the ripped routes' cells); the failed nets are queued first, their blockers after them, and the next round starts. After the second global rip-up the history map is cleared (`self.obstacle_map.clear_history()`) and `ripped_once` is reset, which is the epoch boundary of Milestone 2's once-per-epoch rule; the existing "two rounds without progress" reset stays as a second trigger. The loop ends with success when a round leaves the failed set empty, and with failure after `max_rounds` (default 10, the flow's `--ripup-max-rounds` value is passed through) with `failed_error` naming the remaining nets.
